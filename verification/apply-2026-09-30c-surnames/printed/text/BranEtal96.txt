ABSTRACT:
Long-latency components of event-related potentials (like
the P300 or P3) correlate with the ability of subjects to detect and process
unexpected, novel or task-relevant events. Task-relevant late positive
components were recorded in the neocortex and hippocampus of rats
performing an auditory discrimination task, similar to the “odd-ball” paradigm used in human experiments. Surface and depth electrodes were
implanted in anaesthetized rats at frontal, temporal and anterior occipital neocortical regions and the hippocampus. After recovery from surgery
rats were trained to discriminate two auditory signals, a frequent irrelevant tone and a rare tone related to water reward. In response to the
task-relevant tone but not the irrelevant tone, P300-like late positive components (mean latency of 274 ms) were recorded throughout the surface
of the neocortex. The largest amplitudes were found at the anterior occipital cortex situated above the hippocampal CA1 region. The amplitude
of the task-relevant positive component increased further with cortical
depth without reversing its polarity. An amplitude maximum was found
in the CA1 region with a polarity reversal at the pyramidal cell layer and
the largest negative amplitude in stratum radiatum. Power spectra of differences between responses evoked by task-relevant tones and those
evoked by irrelevant tones revealed peaks i n the theta range (4-12 Hz).
It is suggested that the P300-like component in rats corresponds to a theta
wave out of a burst of hippocampal theta cycles. 0 1996 Wiley-Liss, inc.

The task-relevant late positive component of the human event-related potential (P300 or P3) has been studied extensively since its discovery by Sutton
et al. (1965). It was shown that it depends upon the circumstances under
which stimuli are presented and is rather independent of their physical parameters. The task-relevant late positive component appears to be an electrophysiological correlate of such cognitive finctions as sequential information processing, stimulus discrimination and short-term memory (Pritchard,
1981; Donchin et al., 1986). Although knowledge of its origin would be of
interest both because of its cognitive significance and because of its abnormality in Alzheimer’s disease (Polich, 1989; Kraiuhin et al., 1989) and schizophrenia (Ford et al., 1992), the neural substrate of the P300 is not known.

Generators of the human P300 were postulated to be located in the frontal cortex (Courchesne, 1978; Knight et
al., 1981; Wood and McCarthy, 1985), temporal cortex
(Richer et al., 1983) and the hippocampal formation
(Halgren et al., 1980; Squires et al., 1983; Okada et al.,
1983; Halgren et al., 1986; McCarthy et al., 1989).
P3OO-like potentials have been reported in monkeys
(Neville and Foote, 1984; Pineda et al., 1989), cats
(Wilder et al., 1981; Harrison and Buchwald, 1987;
Basar-Eroglu et al., 1991) and rats (Ehlers et al., 1991;
Yamaguchi et al., 1993; Jodo et al., 1993; Iwanami et
al., 1994). The P300-like potential in cats disappeared
after medial septal lesions (Harrison et al., 1988) but not
after ablation of the primary auditory cortex or the associational cortex (Harrison et al., 1986, 1990). In primates, lesions of the locus coeruleus caused an amplitude
decrease of the P300 (Pineda et al., 1989). The following study was designed to further investigate the neocortical distribution of the rat’s P300 and its source of
generation. Some of these results have been presented in
preliminary form elsewhere (BrankaEk and Seidenbecher,
1995).

Sixteen male Wistar rats weighing between 200 and

300 g were housed individually and allowed ad libitum
access to food. Water was available until behavioral training began, after which the rats received water as reinforcement in the testing apparatus and for 30 min after
each training session.
